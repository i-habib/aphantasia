# Biological routes linking autism and weak imagery: literature review

Search date: 29 September 2026 (web searches; key papers opened where access allowed). Each entry gives what the paper showed and what it means for us. The notes are based on abstracts and search summaries unless marked *(read)*. Check each claim against the full paper before citing it in a manuscript.

## A. Pathway 1: a "noisy" sensory cortex (excitation/inhibition balance)

| Paper | What it showed | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| Keogh, Bergmann & Pearson (2020), *eLife* 9:e50232 | Lower resting activity and excitability in early visual cortex (V1–V3), measured by fMRI, TMS phosphene thresholds and MRS, predicted *stronger* imagery. Higher prefrontal excitability predicted stronger imagery. Proposed mechanism: sensory-cortex hyperexcitability is noise that swamps top-down imagery signals. | The core mechanistic link. If autism raises sensory-cortex excitability, imagery should weaken. |
| Robertson, Ratai & Kanwisher (2016), *Current Biology* 26:80–85 | Autistic adults (n=20 vs 21) showed weaker perceptual suppression in binocular rivalry. Visual-cortex GABA predicted suppression in controls but not in autistic people. | Strongest direct evidence for weaker GABA *action* in autistic visual cortex. Binocular rivalry is also Pearson's standard objective imagery measure, so one lab paradigm could test both. |
| MRS meta-analysis of autism (2024, medRxiv; "Neurometabolite differences in autism…") | Lower GABA overall in autism, strongest in children and limbic regions; **no significant GABA difference in autistic adults**. | A real weakness: the imagery datasets are adults. The pathway may run through GABA *function* (Robertson) rather than GABA *levels*. |
| Motor-cortex MRS–TMS study in young autistic adults (2022, *Frontiers in Psychiatry*) | Higher Glx (glutamate+glutamine), unchanged GABA+. | Fits "more excitation" better than "less inhibition". Keogh also links glutamate to excitability. |
| Taylor et al. (2026), *Scientific Reports* *(read, summary)* | In 595 adults, imagery was only negligibly associated with self-reported sensory sensitivity (Glasgow Sensory Questionnaire). The authors say this challenges a shared-hyperexcitability account. | The main published counterpoint. Its test used a sensory **questionnaire** as a stand-in for cortical excitability, which is our opening. |

## B. Pathway 2: weaker top-down connections (long-range connectivity)

| Paper | What it showed | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| Spagna et al. (2021), *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* (meta-analysis of 46 fMRI studies) | Visual imagery reliably engages frontoparietal networks and a small left fusiform region (the "fusiform imagery node", FIN, MNI −42, −54, −18), but **not early visual cortex**. | Complicates Pathway 1's focus on V1, and points to a specific seed region for Pathway 2. |
| Liu et al. (2025), *Cortex* 185:113–132 (7T fMRI, aphantasia) | Aphantasic people activated visual cortex during imagery, but the FIN was functionally disconnected from left frontoparietal regions (premotor and anterior prefrontal). | The brain "signature" of aphantasia: a weak FIN–prefrontal link. |
| Kutsche, …, Fox & Kletenik (2025–26), medRxiv → *Cortex* ("Lesions causing aphantasia are connected to the fusiform imagery node") | All 12 lesions causing acquired aphantasia were functionally connected to the left FIN. The discussion links this to autism's reduced long-range connectivity. | The connectivity link to autism has been *proposed*, but we found no test of it. |
| Kleinhans et al. (2025), *NeuroSci* *(read, summary)* | In 17 autistic vs 22 control participants, the right fusiform face area was **more** connected to limbic and sensory regions. No imagery. | Small study, different region; no overlap with a FIN test. |
| ABIDE fusiform connectivity studies (e.g., *Sci Rep* 2016 "Alteration of functional connectivity in autism") | Lower fusiform connectivity in autism among other regions. | Fusiform differences in autism exist, but these studies were not framed around imagery or the FIN–prefrontal edge. Before registering, check exact overlap with any study that analysed a seed near −42, −54, −18. |

**Why this may be the better pathway:** the FIN sits right beside the left fusiform face area. Arnold et al. (2026) proposed that face-processing difficulty links autism and aphantasia. A shared FIN/face-area connectivity difference would give one biological account for both.

## C. Sense-specific route: smell

| Paper | What it showed | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| Olfactory function in autism across the lifespan (2026), *Autism* (systematic review and meta-analysis) | Autistic people had poorer odor identification and higher odor thresholds on objective tests and informant reports, but **not** on self-report. | Real smell perception differences exist, which supports Arnold's smell-imagery finding having a sensory basis. Self-report misses them, so the answering-style explanation needs testing too. |
| Meta-analysis of odor thresholds and identification in autism (2017, *PMC5425471*) | Very heterogeneous: both hypo- and hypersensitivity. | Expect noisy smell results. |

## D. Genetics

| Paper | What it showed | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| Zeman et al. (2022), abstract, *JNNP* ("Does visual imagery vividness have a genetic basis? A GWAS of 1,019 individuals") | No significant variants. | No imagery GWAS is usable. Genetic correlation with autism is not feasible without UK Biobank access. |

## E. Methodological warnings (brain-map comparison studies)

| Paper | Point |
|---|---|
| "A critical appraisal of imaging transcriptomics" (PMC9616265); "Statistical testing in transcriptomic-neuroimaging studies" (*HBM* 2022); "Toward best practices for imaging transcriptomics" (*Biol Psychiatry*) | Neighbouring brain regions are similar, so ordinary tests inflate false positives. Spatially aware null models are required. |
| "The effect of spherical projection on spin tests" (*Imaging Neuroscience*, 2025); "A significant enrichment that is not" (bioRxiv 2026) | Even the standard "spin" nulls leak false positives (>40% error with strongly autocorrelated maps). Only ~3% of single-gene associations survive proper correction. |
| Romero-Garcia et al. (2019), *Molecular Psychiatry* | Already linked autism cortical-thickness differences to synaptic and downregulated genes. An autism-map-to-gene analysis alone is **not new**. |

## F. Other

- Wei et al. (2025), *Autism Research*: review of visual imagery in autism, covering aphantasia and enhanced imagery abilities. The main existing review, and a competitor for any theory paper.
- King et al. (2024), *Consciousness and Cognition*: autistic adults reported lower visual and emotional imagery; about 20% vs 6% met aphantasia criteria.
- Milton et al. (2021), *Cerebral Cortex Communications*: aphantasic people had higher AQ scores; relevant fMRI signatures of imagery extremes.

## Implications for the programme

1. **Brain-maps overlap (P6 as first proposed) is weak as a standalone paper.** The ENIGMA autism map has only 68 regions, and the public imagery map is sparse (see feasibility, README). Spatial-overlap tests are under heavy criticism, and the autism-to-gene half has been done. It is still useful as a supplementary analysis.
2. **FIN–prefrontal connectivity in ABIDE is the strongest public-data biological test.** It uses individual brains from a large sample, asks a pre-specified question taken from the aphantasia literature, and appears untested. It tests Pathway 2. ABIDE has no imagery measure, so the claim is "autistic brains show the connectivity signature of aphantasia", not a direct imagery link.
3. **P5 (questionnaire pattern)** tests the same two pathways behaviourally. An even drop across all senses fits top-down Pathway 2; extra smell/taste loss fits a sensory route.
4. **The decisive test needs a lab:** binocular-rivalry imagery priming plus visual-cortex MRS, in autistic vs non-autistic adults, as a Robertson × Pearson design. Pitch this to a collaborator after P5/P7.
