P5 Questionnaire study: autism, ADHD and multisensory imagery

Private OSF draft · not registered · open in OSF

1 · Metadata

Title
Autism and weaker multisensory imagery: testing Arnold et al. (2026) in an independent sample, with ADHD for comparison
Description
Arnold et al. (2026) found that autistic adults reported less vivid imagery across the senses, and were more often unable to imagine smells and tastes, though not more often unable to picture images. These results come from a single survey, and earlier studies of autism and imagery disagree with each other, so an independent check is worth doing. New et al. (2026) ran two large online imagery surveys. In the second, 1,003 adults also said whether they had been diagnosed with, or strongly suspected they had, autism, ADHD or a learning condition. New et al. only reported the combined any-condition group. We compare the 150 people who reported autism and the 84 who reported ADHD without autism with the 749 who reported no condition. We ask whether the autism group has weaker imagery overall and is more often unable to imagine smells and tastes, and whether the ADHD group shows the same pattern, which would suggest the effect is not specific to autism. Because power is moderate, we also set out how we will interpret a null result.

2 · Study Information

Research questions
Arnold et al. (2026) surveyed 327 adults with a formal autism diagnosis and compared them with 327 people from an earlier survey. The autistic group reported less vivid imagery across the senses on average. They were not more likely to say they had no visual imagery, but they were more likely to say they could not imagine smells or tastes. This has not been checked in a second sample.

New et al. (2026) ran two online surveys of imagery in eight senses. The second survey (their "Study 2"; 1,003 adults after their attention check) also asked whether people had been diagnosed with, or strongly suspected they had, a neurodevelopmental condition, and if so which one. New et al. analysed only the combined any-condition group, not autism or ADHD on their own.

1. In New et al.'s second survey, do people who reported autism show the pattern Arnold et al. found: weaker imagery overall, and more often no smell or taste imagery?
2. If so, is this specific to autism, or do people who reported ADHD (without autism) show it too?
Hypotheses
Groups. The autism group is everyone who ticked autism (some also ticked ADHD). The ADHD group ticked ADHD but not autism. The comparison group said they had no neurodevelopmental condition.

Main hypotheses, taken from Arnold et al.:
H1. The autism group has a lower overall imagery score than the comparison group. The overall score is the average across the six senses both studies measured: sight, sound, smell, taste, touch and movement.
H2a. More people in the autism group have no smell imagery at all (the lowest possible score).
H2b. The same for taste.

Secondary hypotheses:
S1. The autism group's gap is bigger for smell and taste than for sight.
S2. Whether the autism and ADHD groups differ in overall imagery. This tells us whether any effect is specific to autism. We make no prediction about its direction.
S3. Whether the autism group is more likely to have no visual imagery. Arnold et al. found no difference, so we expect none.
S4. Whether the ADHD group shows the same smell-and-taste versus sight pattern as in S1. No prediction.

Data collection procedures
We collected no new data. New et al. ran their second survey online. The file includes Prolific IDs, so participants appear to have been recruited through Prolific. The survey covered imagery in eight senses and included attention checks.

The neurodevelopmental question read: "Have you ever been diagnosed with, or strongly suspected to have, a neurodevelopmental disorder such as Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), or a learning disability?" (No/Yes). People who answered Yes then ticked any that applied: ASD, ADHD, dyslexia, dyscalculia, dysgraphia, language disorder, dyspraxia, other. This wording comes from the questionnaire PDF in New et al.'s OSF project (Study 2, pages 3 and 4). Both papers describe their methods in full.
Codebook
Imagery scores in New et al.'s survey: each sense has five questions rated 1 to 7, added up to a score from 5 to 35 (higher means more vivid). Matched to Arnold et al.'s six senses: sight = image, sound = sound, smell = smell, taste = taste, touch = touch, movement = motor. New et al. also measured emotion, voice and inner-body (intstate) imagery. Arnold et al. did not, so we only describe these.

Groups come from two columns: isneurodev (the yes/no question) and neurodev2 (the conditions ticked). We also use age and sex, coded 0, 1 and O. The three people coded O stay in as their own category.

No imagery in a sense means the lowest score, 5: the bottom answer on all five questions. This is New et al.'s own definition. Arnold et al.'s equivalent is a score of 0.

4 · Variables

Manipulated variables
None. This is an observational secondary analysis of existing data.
Measured variables
Group: autism, ADHD without autism, or no condition (see Hypotheses). The question combined diagnosis with strong suspicion, so our autism group is people who reported diagnosed or suspected autism. Arnold et al. required a formal diagnosis, and we will discuss this difference.

Outcomes:
- Each sense's imagery score, rescaled so the comparison group has an average of 0 and a standard deviation (SD) of 1. This puts all senses, and both studies, on the same scale.
- Overall imagery: the average of the six rescaled sense scores, rescaled again the same way. A group difference of -0.2 means that group scores 0.2 SD below the comparison group.
- Whether a person has no imagery for smell, for taste, and for sight.
Arnold et al.'s data are rescaled the same way against their comparison group.

Covariates: age and sex. We do not adjust for education or occupation, because autism can affect both, and Arnold et al. did not adjust for them.
Missing data
One person has no recorded age. They are left out of the analyses that adjust for age and kept in the simple descriptive ones. We do not fill in missing values, since this affects fewer than 0.1% of people. No imagery scores are missing; if any were, that person would have no overall score.
Unit of analysis
Each person. In S1 and S4 each person contributes six scores, one per sense, and the analysis accounts for this.
Statistical outliers
We remove no outliers. The questionnaire limits scores to 5 to 35, so a value outside that range would mean a data error, and the analysis code stops rather than dropping it.
Sampling weights
None.

5 · Knowledge of Data

Prior publication / dissemination
New et al. (2026) reported how common very weak and very strong imagery are in each sense, and compared people with and without any neurodevelopmental condition as one group. They did not look at autism or ADHD separately. Arnold et al. (2026) reported their own results. Their comparisons of how many people lack imagery used only the 242 autistic participants who scored 26 or more on the Autism Spectrum Quotient, whereas we use all 327.
Prior knowledge
We have read both papers and reanalysed Arnold et al.'s data, using all 327 autistic participants and their 242-person subset. We used those results to estimate power.

We have used New et al.'s second survey before, for a different question: whether imagery relates to currently being treated for a neurological or psychiatric condition. One model in that work used the combined any-condition group as its outcome. People with more vivid imagery were slightly less likely to report a condition (visual imagery odds ratio 0.86, 95% CI 0.70 to 1.05; other senses 0.89, 0.73 to 1.08). That group mixes autism, ADHD and learning conditions, but it hints at the direction of H1, so we report it here.

For this study we have looked only at the questionnaire wording, the condition codes and the size of each group. We have not compared imagery between the autism, ADHD and comparison groups. The analysis code will not run until this plan is registered.

6 · Analyses

Statistical models
Main analyses

H1, overall imagery. We compare the autism and comparison groups with a linear regression: overall imagery is the outcome, with age and sex as covariates. Standard errors are robust to unequal variance (HC3). We predict the autism group scores lower, so the test is one-sided.

H2a and H2b, no smell or no taste imagery. We use logistic regression of "no smell imagery" (then "no taste imagery") on group, age and sex, with robust standard errors. We predict higher odds in the autism group (one-sided). If one group has very few such cases, the standard model can fail, and we then use Firth's corrected logistic regression. If fewer than 10 people across the two groups have no imagery in that sense, we use Fisher's exact test instead, without covariates.

Order of testing. We test H1 first, then H2a, then H2b, each at the 5% level. A hypothesis only counts as supported if all the ones before it were. This keeps the overall chance of a false positive across the three at 5%. H1 comes first because it is Arnold et al.'s broadest finding and the one we have the most power to detect. We report all three results either way.

Secondary analyses (two-sided, with a Holm correction across the five tests below)

S1. Using all six sense scores for each person, we estimate how the autism group's gap varies by sense, with sight as the baseline. We test (a) whether the average of the smell and taste gaps is bigger than the sight gap, and (b) whether the gap differs across the six senses at all. Standard errors account for each person contributing six scores.
S2. One regression comparing both the autism and ADHD groups with the comparison group. We test whether the autism and ADHD groups differ from each other, and also report the ADHD group's difference from the comparison group.
S3. As H2, for sight.
S4. As S1(a), for the ADHD group.

Comparison with Arnold et al. We run the H1 and S1 analyses on Arnold et al.'s data in the same way and show the two studies side by side, with a test of whether they differ. We do not combine them into one estimate, because the studies used different questionnaires, recruitment and definitions of autism.

All results are reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Code: Python (NumPy, pandas, SciPy), file src/aphantasia/p5.py at https://github.com/i-habib/aphantasia. Full plan: preregistration.md (SHA-256 8a90ff1edef1573660b29e90666d1e8eae8f369cab70b2a9cdec97a9c0d57635).
Effect size
H1: the difference in overall imagery, in comparison-group SDs. H2: the odds ratio for having no imagery in that sense.

For planning we used Arnold et al.'s results for all 327 autistic adults: overall imagery 0.22 SD lower; the smell-and-taste gap 0.13 SD bigger than the sight gap; no smell imagery in 7.3% versus 2.4% (odds ratio about 3.2); no taste imagery in 6.1% versus 2.4% (about 2.6). First studies tend to overestimate effects, so we also plan for an effect half that size.
Statistical power
The group sizes are set by the data: 150 autism, 84 ADHD, 749 comparison. We estimated power from Arnold et al.'s effects and these group sizes, using a one-sided 5% test:
- H1: 79% if the true effect matches Arnold et al.'s, 34% if it is half as large.
- H1 using only the 104 autistic people who did not also report ADHD: 67% and 27%.
- S1: 63% and 25%.
- H2a and H2b: 87% if the odds ratio is 3, 46% if it is 2, assuming the comparison group's rate is 2.4% as in Arnold et al.
Power is limited if the true effect is smaller than Arnold et al. found, so we explain below how we will read a non-significant result.
Inference criteria
H1 has three possible outcomes. To judge a null result we use Simonsohn's (2015) "small telescopes" approach, which asks whether the original study could have detected an effect as small as ours. We call d33 the effect Arnold et al.'s study had a 33% chance of detecting, about 0.12 SD.
1. Replicated: H1 is significant (one-sided p below .05).
2. Failed to replicate: H1 is not significant, and our estimate is significantly smaller than d33. Any real effect would then be too small for Arnold et al.'s study to have picked up reliably.
3. Inconclusive: neither. We report the range of effects our data cannot rule out.

H2 counts as a replication only if it passes the order of testing described under Statistical models. Otherwise we report its odds ratio next to Arnold et al.'s.

We call a pattern specific to autism only if the autism and ADHD groups differ significantly (S2). A near-zero result for the ADHD group does not show this on its own.

These are associations in a self-selected online sample, and we make no causal claims.
Assumption violation / model non-convergence
If a logistic model fails because a group has too few cases, it switches to Firth's correction, and with fewer than 10 cases to Fisher's exact test (see Statistical models). Robust standard errors are used throughout, so unequal variance needs no special handling. If a model still cannot be fitted, we report that and why, rather than swapping in a different model. Any change from this plan goes in a dated log that notes whether we had seen results, and is reported in the paper.
Reliability and robustness testing
We repeat the analyses in a few different ways and report all of them alongside the main results:
1. H1, H2a and H2b using only the 104 autistic people who did not also report ADHD.
2. H1 using the plain average of the six raw scores, without rescaling.
3. H1 using only people aged 18 to 30, the age range New et al. recruited.
4. H1 without adjusting for age and sex.
5. S1 allowing the effects of age and sex to differ by sense.
Exploratory analysis
We describe, without testing, the group differences in each sense (Hedges' g for the autism and ADHD groups versus the comparison group). This includes emotion, voice and inner-body imagery, which only New et al. measured. We also give the number of people with no imagery in each sense and group, showing counts below 5 as "fewer than 5". Anything else will be labelled exploratory in the paper.

P7 Brain connectivity study: fusiform imagery node in ABIDE

Private OSF draft · not registered · open in OSF

1 · Metadata

Title
Do autistic brains show the connection pattern seen in aphantasia? Fusiform imagery node connectivity in ABIDE I
Description
Visual imagery relies on a small area of the left fusiform gyrus, the fusiform imagery node (FIN), working with areas in the left parietal and frontal lobes. In people with lifelong aphantasia, the FIN still responds during imagery, but its link with the left frontal lobe is weaker. Autistic adults report weaker imagery on average, and autism has long been linked to differences in long-range brain connections, but no one has checked whether autistic people show this aphantasia pattern. We use resting-state brain scans from ABIDE I, a public dataset of about 1,000 autistic and non-autistic people from 20 sites. We test whether the FIN is less connected to three left parietal and frontal imagery areas in autistic people, and whether that difference is larger than any general difference in the FIN's connections with the rest of the brain. ABIDE has no imagery questionnaire, so a positive result would show a shared brain feature, not that this feature causes weaker imagery.

2 · Study Information

Research questions
A review of 27 brain-imaging studies (Spagna et al., 2021) found that visual imagery reliably activates a small area of the left fusiform gyrus, the fusiform imagery node (FIN), along with areas in the left parietal and frontal lobes. In people with lifelong aphantasia, the FIN still responds during imagery, but it is less connected to left frontal areas, especially dorsal premotor and prefrontal cortex (Liu et al., 2025; 10 people with aphantasia and 10 without). Brain injuries that cause aphantasia occur in areas connected to the FIN (Kutsche et al., 2025/2026).

Autistic adults report weaker imagery on average (King et al., 2024; Arnold et al., 2026; Taylor et al., 2026), and autism has long been linked to differences in long-range brain connections. Kutsche et al. suggested the two might be related, but we found no study that tested it.

1. In ABIDE I, is the FIN less connected to left parietal and frontal imagery areas in autistic than in non-autistic people?
2. If so, is this specific to those imagery connections, or part of a general difference in how the FIN connects to the rest of the brain?

ABIDE has no imagery measure, so a positive result would show that autistic brains share a feature of aphantasia on average. It would not show that this feature causes weaker imagery.
Hypotheses
H1 (main). Autistic people have weaker average connectivity between the FIN and three left-hemisphere imagery areas: parietal, dorsal premotor, and precentral/middle frontal. The exact locations are under Measured variables.

H2 (specificity). This difference is bigger than the difference in the FIN's average connectivity with the rest of the brain.

We also look at each of the three connections separately, compare the left hemisphere with the matching right-hemisphere areas, and repeat H1 in adults only.

We expect weaker connectivity, because that is what the aphantasia studies found. We still test in both directions, because autism studies have found both weaker and stronger connections depending on age and brain region. A stronger connection would count against our hypothesis.

Data collection procedures
We collected no new data. Each of the 20 ABIDE I sites scanned autistic and non-autistic people at rest, using its own scanner, procedures and questionnaires, and shared the data through ABIDE. The Preprocessed Connectomes Project then processed every scan the same way. We use their C-PAC output, which corrects for head motion, removes noise signals, filters the data, aligns each brain to a standard template (MNI) and averages the signal within each atlas region. Di Martino et al. (2014) and Craddock et al. (2013) give the details.
Codebook
From the participant file we use: DX_GROUP (1 autistic, 2 non-autistic), AGE_AT_SCAN, SEX (1 male, 2 female), SITE_ID, EYE_STATUS_AT_SCAN (1 open, 2 closed), func_mean_fd (average head movement during the scan, in mm), the three functional scan-quality ratings (qc_rater_1, qc_func_rater_2, qc_func_rater_3), and FIQ (IQ). For the exploratory analyses we also use ADOS_TOTAL, SRS_RAW_TOTAL and AQ_TOTAL. Negative values in IQ and these scores mean the value is missing.

Each time-series file has one row per time point and one column per atlas region, headed by the region number (for example #296). The CC400 files have 392 regions.

4 · Variables

Manipulated variables
None. This is an observational secondary analysis of existing data.
Measured variables
Group: autistic or not (DX_GROUP).

Brain areas, all peak locations from Spagna et al. (2021), in MNI coordinates (x, y, z):
- FIN, left fusiform gyrus: -42, -54, -18
- Left parietal (inferior parietal lobule / intraparietal sulcus): -40, -44, 46
- Left dorsal premotor / middle frontal gyrus: -26, -2, 60. Liu et al. (2025) found this the most consistently weakened connection in aphantasia.
- Left precentral / middle frontal gyrus, near the inferior frontal junction: -52, 10, 38
- Right fusiform, mirroring the FIN: 42, -54, -18
- Right parietal (inferior parietal lobule / precuneus): 40, -42, 48
- Right middle frontal / precentral gyrus: 30, -4, 54
We leave out Spagna et al.'s other peaks (supplementary motor area, anterior insula), because no aphantasia study has linked them to the FIN.

Each location is matched to the CC400 region that contains it, or the nearest region within 6 mm; if there is none, the analysis stops. We did this matching before registering, using only the atlas. All seven locations fall inside different regions in both atlases, and all seven regions appear in every scan file.

Connectivity between two regions is the correlation of their signals over the scan, converted to Fisher's z so that values can be averaged.
- Main outcome: the average connectivity between the FIN and the three left-hemisphere areas.
- Specificity outcome: the main outcome minus the FIN's average connectivity with all other regions. We leave out the three target areas and any region within 20 mm of the FIN, because neighbouring regions share signal.
- Hemisphere outcome: the main outcome minus the average of the two right-hemisphere connections.

Covariates: age (and age squared, to allow a curve), sex, head movement, whether eyes were open or closed (this affects connectivity in visual areas), and scanning site. We do not adjust for IQ in the main analysis, because it is missing at several sites and may itself be affected by autism. We adjust for it in a robustness check.
Missing data
People missing age, sex or eye status are left out. IQ and symptom scores are used only in the robustness and exploratory analyses, with whoever has them. We do not fill in missing values.
Unit of analysis
Each person (one scan per person).
Statistical outliers
We remove people in this order and report how many at each step:
1. No scan file.
2. Average head movement above 0.2 mm.
3. A rater marked the scan as failed.
4. No usable signal in one of the seven regions.
5. Missing age, sex or eye status.
6. Sites left with fewer than 5 autistic or 5 non-autistic people, since a site needs both groups to contribute a comparison.
We remove no other outliers. So far we have only counted people: 884 of the 1,035 with scans pass step 2 (not split by group), and 317 are 18 or older.
Sampling weights
None.

5 · Knowledge of Data

Prior publication / dissemination
Many studies have analysed ABIDE I connectivity, and some report differences in fusiform connectivity in autism. We found none that looked at the FIN's connections with these frontal and parietal areas, or that framed the question around imagery. Before submitting the paper, we will check for any ABIDE study that used a starting point near (-42, -54, -18) and explain how ours differs.
Prior knowledge
We know the aphantasia and autism research summarised above, including that ABIDE connectivity findings in autism are mixed and vary with age.

Before registering, we looked only at: the participant file's column names; how many people there are by group and site; head movement, scan-quality ratings and ages across everyone (not by group); the two atlases, to match the brain locations; the scan-file headers, to check every region is present; and Spagna et al.'s tables.

We have not calculated any connectivity values or compared autistic and non-autistic people on any brain measure. The analysis code will not run until this plan is registered, and its tests use simulated data only.

6 · Analyses

Statistical models
Main analysis (H1). A linear regression with the main outcome as the outcome and autism as the predictor, adjusting for age, age squared, sex, head movement, eye status and site. Standard errors are robust to unequal variance (HC3). We report the autism difference with a 95% confidence interval and a two-sided p-value at the 5% level. We also report it as a standardized difference: the difference divided by the outcome's within-group SD.

Secondary analyses. The same model with five other outcomes, Holm-corrected as a set:
1. the specificity outcome (H2);
2 to 4. each of the three FIN connections on its own;
5. the hemisphere outcome.

We also report two analyses outside that set: H1 in adults only, since the imagery research is in adults; and H1 with an autism-by-age term, to see whether the difference changes with age.

Code: Python (NumPy, pandas, SciPy), file src/aphantasia/p7.py at https://github.com/i-habib/aphantasia. Full plan: preregistration.md (SHA-256 337b7efe5f70b3f756950d5ea9d2005bfb88b724401217671191658e55e34e7c).
Effect size
The autism difference in connectivity (in Fisher's z), and the same difference divided by the within-group SD. We treat a standardized difference of 0.15 as the smallest that matters. This is smaller than the autism versus non-autism differences usually reported in ABIDE.
Statistical power
We expect around 400 people per group after exclusions. With a two-sided 5% test, that gives about 80% power for a standardized difference of 0.20 and about 55% for 0.15. Adjusting for site and the other covariates will change this a little. The adults-only analysis (about 250 people) has much less power, so we treat it as descriptive.
Inference criteria
How we will read the results:
1. H1 and H2 both show weaker connectivity, with confidence intervals that exclude zero. The FIN is less connected to the imagery areas in autism, beyond any general difference. This fits the idea that autistic brains share aphantasia's connection pattern.
2. H1 shows weaker connectivity but H2 does not. The FIN is less connected, but no more than to the rest of the brain, which points to a general difference rather than one specific to imagery.
3. H1's confidence interval includes zero. There is no sign of the aphantasia pattern. We then test whether we can rule out differences as large as 0.15 SD, using an equivalence test (two one-sided tests).
4. H1 shows stronger connectivity. This counts against the idea.
Assumption violation / model non-convergence
If a brain location is more than 6 mm from every atlas region, or two locations land in the same region, the analysis stops and we record the change. People with no signal in a needed region are removed (step 4 under Statistical outliers). Robust standard errors handle unequal variance. If a model cannot be fitted, we report that and why. Any change from this plan goes in a dated log that notes whether we had seen results, and is reported in the paper.
Reliability and robustness testing
We repeat the main analysis in these ways and report all of them:
1. With global signal regression, a common but debated processing step.
2. With a stricter head-movement limit (0.1 mm), and with no movement limit.
3. With the CC200 atlas instead of CC400.
4. Adjusting for IQ, in people who have an IQ score.
5. Males only, since most ABIDE I participants are male.
6. Leaving out one site at a time, and reporting the range of results.
7. Handling site with clustered standard errors instead of site terms in the model.
Exploratory analysis
Within autistic people, we look at whether the main outcome relates to autism symptom scores (ADOS and SRS totals, where at least 30 people have them) and, at the few sites that collected it, the AQ. We also map how the FIN's connections to every region differ between the groups. These are exploratory, and we draw no firm conclusions from them.
